01 · the problem

Reading the steps for a parallel park is easy. Doing them in the right order, in a real car, with an examiner beside you, is not.

  • Handbooks are lists

    The official guide explains each maneuver as text. Learners memorise it, then freeze when they have to steer, check and brake at the same time.

  • Videos are passive

    Watching someone else reverse into a space doesn't build the habit of signalling first and looking over your shoulder.

  • Small misses cost marks

    A skipped shoulder check, a rolling stop or a tyre on the curb can lose points or end the test early.

goal

Turn each maneuver into something you drive yourself, with the examiner's checks built into every step.

02 · process

Six rounds of try it, fix it

Every round started from using the prototype on my phone and writing down what felt wrong. Here is what changed and why.

1
first idea

An animated explainer

The first version was a desktop page: pick a maneuver, press Play, and watch a top-down car perform it while a card lists the checks for each step.

what I learnedIt looked tidy but felt like a website. Watching still isn't doing, and learners practise on their phones, not laptops.
2
layout

Turn the road up the phone

I rethought the screen as a phone in portrait: the road runs from top to bottom, with two parked cars on the right and your car about to reverse between them.

That split the screen into three zones that every later version kept. The step to do sits on top, the road fills the middle, and every control sits where the thumbs rest.

design rulePhone first, portrait only, and the person drives. No Play button.
3
interaction model

Controls you'd find in a car

I replaced the Play button with the four things a learner actually touches: a steering wheel you drag (one and a half turns each way), a P / R / D selector, a pedal you hold to creep, and buttons for the signal and each shoulder check.

Each step lists what it needs as small chips. A chip fills in the moment you do it, and when every chip is filled the next step starts on its own.

why chips?They show progress without a "Next" button, so you never skip ahead by accident.
4
testing

"I can't see anything"

The first mistake message was a big card that slid over the road. On a phone it covered the car, the curb and the reason you'd failed.

It went through two shrinks before landing as a slim strip pinned to the top of the road: one line of advice, plus Retry and Restart.

fixFeedback should point at the road, not cover it.
5
testing

"Retry doesn't work"

I did every check and nothing happened, and Retry seemed dead. The cause was a rule that fired on the very first frame: the car started close enough to the curb to count as "pulling over before signalling", so the step failed again the moment Retry reset it.

The controls also kept working while the game was paused, so chips ticked green even though nothing could move on.

fixOnly judge a move after the car actually moves. While a mistake is showing, grey out the controls and mark the strip with a "!".
6
visual direction

From slick to scribbled

I tried a sleek black-and-lime fintech look from a reference, then went back to black and white. Neither felt like learning to drive. It felt like a dashboard.

The direction that stuck came from a child's drawing of a car: wobbly pencil lines, a big obvious front, and a couple of colours filled in with crayon. I tightened the lines so it reads as neat, not messy, then tested colour pairs until crayon orange and blue felt right.

why doodle?A test is stressful. A drawing you could have made yourself makes practice feel low-stakes, and the bold front of the car makes direction obvious at a glance.
03 · final design

Five maneuvers, one rhythm

Three-point turn, parallel parking, roadside stop, lane change and left turn at a light. Every one uses the same loop: read the step, do the checks, drive, stop.

How a step works

read the step→ signal + look→ steer · gear · hold→ all chips filled → next step
↳ mistake → strip → Retry this step

Each step has its own checks (for example "Signal right", "Right shoulder check", "Stop by the curb") and its own guard rules (for example "don't move toward the curb before signalling"). Retry rewinds to where that step began, not to the start of the maneuver.

05 · reflection

What I'd do next

What I learned

  • The biggest fixes came from using it, not from planning it. The covered road and the "dead" Retry button were obvious on a phone and invisible on a laptop.
  • A rule that judges the player has to wait for the player to act. Otherwise it feels broken, even when it's technically right.
  • Style changed how the task feels. The same steps felt like a test in black and lime, and like practice in crayon.

Next steps

  • Test with five learners booked for a G2 test and time how long each maneuver takes them.
  • Add "full left / full right" shortcuts beside the wheel, since one and a half turns is a lot of dragging on a small screen.
  • Add a roadside-stop-to-highway merge and a school-zone scenario.
  • Add haptics when the car touches the curb.
